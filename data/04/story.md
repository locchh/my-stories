Minh's apartment gave him almost every **convenience** he could ask for. His office was fifteen minutes away, the hospital never closed, and a bowl of noodles could arrive at his door after midnight. Everything he needed was nearby, yet the one thing he wanted most always seemed distant.

He wanted quiet.

Every morning began with motorbikes below his window. Every evening ended with construction from the building next door. The city made his life efficient, but it left little room for **comfort**. Even on Sundays, when the traffic became lighter, Minh could still feel the noise waiting to return.

## A weekend outside the city

One Friday, Minh visited his aunt in a small village. There were no delivery apps, no cafés open at midnight, and no hospital around the corner. However, he slept without earplugs for the first time in months. In the morning, he opened the window and heard only birds moving between the trees.

For a few hours, he imagined moving there permanently. Then his aunt asked him to drive her to a medical appointment. The journey took more than an hour in each direction. On the way home, Minh understood the **trade-off**: the village returned his peace but collected payment in time and distance.

He began to see the decision as a kind of **arithmetic**. Living in the city subtracted quiet but added access. Living in the countryside added space but subtracted speed. Neither answer was wrong; each simply used a different formula.

## The neighbourhood everyone discovered

A month later, an estate agent showed Minh a peaceful neighbourhood on the edge of the city. It appeared to offer everything: fresh air, a short commute, and enough shops to make daily life easy. Minh thought he had finally escaped the choice.

But hundreds of other people had reached the same conclusion. Cars already formed a **queue** at the only road into the area, and new apartment towers were rising beside the park. Its quiet was a shared **resource**, becoming smaller as more residents arrived to enjoy it.

The agent called the neighbourhood a **positional good**—something valuable partly because few people could have it. Once everyone discovered the shortcut, it was no longer short. Once everyone moved toward the quiet street, the street was no longer quiet.

Minh did not buy the apartment. Instead, he stayed in the city and changed how he used it. He chose a smaller home near a park, worked remotely twice a week, and visited his aunt more often. It was not a perfect solution, but it gave him an **exchange rate** he could accept: enough convenience for his work and enough quiet for his mind.

He stopped searching for a place where everything was free. Every place sent a bill. The important question was which price he was willing to pay.
